What if Bayesian policy search knew it was solving an MDP?

TL;DR

Augmented Bayesian Search (ABS) gives Bayesian policy search a prior that knows it is solving an MDP. Through the performance difference lemma, a critic of the current policy predicts the returns of nearby policies, and the gradient the search follows becomes the deterministic policy gradient, corrected by the observed returns. ABS explores with deterministic policies only and outperforms previous Bayesian optimization methods, most clearly in high dimensions.

How it works

Reinforcement learning usually explores with stochastic policies, yet deterministic policies are preferable on physical systems because they avoid erratic, potentially damaging behavior and are easier to implement and interpret. Bayesian optimization explores with deterministic policies by modeling the return as a function of the policy parameters. ABS builds on MPD (Maximum Probability of Descent), a local variant. Around a central policy $\theta$, MPD rolls out the acquisition points $z$ that are most informative about the gradient at $\theta$, steps $\theta$ along the most probable ascent direction, and repeats. Like most Bayesian optimization, it treats the return as a black box and discards the trajectories behind each return. The steps below show how ABS turns those trajectories into a prior and what that prior does to the gradient.

The gradient of a GP is a GP

MPD models the return as a Gaussian process over policy parameters, with prior mean $m$ and kernel $K$. The derivative of a Gaussian process is again a Gaussian process, so after observing returns $Y$ at points $X$, the gradient at $\theta$ has a Gaussian posterior $\mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta, \Sigma_\theta)$. Its mean starts from the gradient of the prior, $\nabla_\theta m(\theta)$, and corrects it where the prior misses the observations. The usual prior is constant, so that first term vanishes and the data must do all the work. A prior that already knows the slope of the return would give a good gradient from few rollouts.

$$ \begin{aligned} \mu_\theta &= \nabla_\theta m(\theta) + \nabla_\theta K(\theta, X)\,K(X, X)^{-1}\big(Y - m(X)\big), \\ \Sigma_\theta &= \nabla_\theta K(\theta, \theta) - \nabla_\theta K(\theta, X)\,K(X, X)^{-1}\,\nabla_\theta K(X, \theta) \end{aligned} $$

Performance difference lemma

The performance difference lemma provides such a prior. It writes the return of any policy $\pi_x$ as the return of the current policy $\pi_\theta$ plus the advantage of $\pi_\theta$, averaged over the states that $\pi_x$ visits. Everything on the right concerns $\pi_\theta$, whose trajectories we have, except the state distribution $d^{\pi_x}$ of a policy we have not run.

$$ J(\pi_x) = J(\pi_\theta) + \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\pi_x}}\big[A^{\pi_\theta}(s, \pi_x(s))\big] $$

Advantage mean function

ABS rolls out the central point $\theta$, collects its return $\widehat{J}(\pi_\theta)$ and states $\widehat{d}^{\pi_\theta}$, and trains a critic $\widehat{Q}^{\pi_\theta}\phi$ on its transitions, which gives the advantage $\widehat{A}^{\pi\theta}\phi$. Replacing the unknown $d^{\pi_x}$ by $\widehat{d}^{\pi\theta}$ leaves a residual, which the paper bounds in Lipschitz MDPs by how far the actions of the two policies differ. Near $\theta$ the residual is small, so the remaining term is a good prior for the return of any point $x$. It acts like a first-order expansion of the return around $\theta$, and the Gaussian process only has to model what it misses.

$$ \widehat{m}_\phi(x) = \widehat{J}(\pi_\theta) + \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \widehat{d}^{\pi_\theta}}\big[\widehat{A}^{\pi_\theta}_\phi(s, \pi_x(s))\big] $$

Fitness-aware policy gradient

The gradient of this prior at $\theta$ is the deterministic policy gradient of the critic. The posterior mean is therefore that policy gradient plus a correction that grows with how far the prior misses the observed returns. Where the critic predicts the returns well, ABS follows the policy gradient, and where it does not, the observed returns take over. One estimate thus connects Bayesian optimization and policy gradient methods.

$$ \begin{aligned} \mu_\theta = {}& \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{s \sim \widehat{d}^{\pi_\theta}}\big[\nabla_a \widehat{Q}^{\pi_\theta}_\phi(s, a)\big|_{a=\pi_\theta(s)}\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(s)\big]}_{\text{deterministic policy gradient}} \\ &+ \underbrace{\nabla_\theta K(\theta, X)\,K(X, X)^{-1}\big(\widehat{J}(X) - \widehat{m}_\phi(X)\big)}_{\text{correction from the observed returns}} \end{aligned} $$

A criterion to validate critics

The same lemma also measures how good a critic is. Rolling out any point $x$ gives its return $\widehat{J}(\pi_x)$ and its own states $\widehat{d}^{\pi_x}$. On those states the lemma holds exactly, so a good critic should reproduce the observed return, and the prediction error tests the critic on trajectories it was not trained on. Every acquisition thus becomes validation data, and ABS uses this score to weight an ensemble of critics.

$$ \begin{aligned} \widetilde{m}_\phi(x) &= \widehat{J}(\pi_\theta) + \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \widehat{d}^{\pi_x}}\big[\widehat{A}^{\pi_\theta}_\phi(s, \pi_x(s))\big], \\ \text{error}_\phi(x) &= \big(\widetilde{m}_\phi(x) - \widehat{J}(\pi_x)\big)^2 \end{aligned} $$

Results

We compare ABS with MPD and with Augmented Random Search (ARS) on MuJoCo locomotion tasks of increasing dimension.

  • The next query lies in the direction of the gradient posterior.
  • The posterior mean $\mu_\theta$ (violet) corrects the critic’s gradient (pink) where the prior misses the observations. Queries stay close to $\theta$, where the prior is reliable.
  • The critic-based prior explains a substantial share of the observed returns.
  • Validation tracks test as in supervised learning, and the prior only needs to find one good ascent direction at a time.
  • ABS consistently outperforms MPD, and its advantage grows in high dimensions.
  • Where MPD and ARS struggle to find good ascent directions, the advantage mean function focuses the search on promising regions of the parameter space.
  • Validation weighting with resets beats every other variant.
  • ABS weights its critics by their validation score and resets the worst one. The score recognizes a freshly reset critic and ignores it until it has learned, which makes resetting safe.

Final remarks

  • The advantage mean function acts like a first-order Taylor expansion of the policy return, bridging Bayesian optimization and reinforcement learning.
  • ABS explores with deterministic policies, learns effectively in an episodic setting, and lets one trade compute for learning speed.
