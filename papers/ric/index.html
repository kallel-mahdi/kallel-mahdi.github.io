Do Not Imitate, Reinforce

Iterative Classification via Belief Refinement

Mahdi Kallel1, Johannes Tölle2, Ahmed Hendawy3, Carlo D'Eramo1

1Center for Artificial Intelligence and Data Science, University of Würzburg 2Automation and Robotics Research Group, SnT, University of Luxembourg 3Department of Computer Science, Technical University of Darmstadt

Paper OpenReview Code
Today classifiers imitate their labels in a single pass and learn to be certain. What if classification used the full strength of reinforcement learning?

TL;DR

Reinforced Iterative Classification (RIC) rewards a classifier for improving its prediction instead of training it to imitate its labels. A recurrent actor refines its prediction step by step and earns a reward for every step toward the label, and a critic decides when to stop. Learning from returns gives fundamentally different learning and generalization dynamics from supervised training, and a better balance of accuracy and calibration across ten image benchmarks.

How it works

Early learning systems learned by trial and error, improving a little at a time. As Andrew Barto put it at RLC 2024, “in the beginning, machine learning was reinforcement learning.” RIC brings this view back to classification. Each labeled example becomes a short episode. The prediction starts uniform over the $K$ classes, and at every step a recurrent network reads the input and the current prediction, a policy head proposes the next class distribution, and a value head estimates whether refining further is worth it. The four steps below show what this agent is trained for and why it learns differently from cross-entropy.

Reward every improvement

Cross-entropy tells the model what to output. RIC only tells it how much its last step helped. The reward is the change in log probability that the prediction assigns to the true label $y$, positive for a step toward $y$ and negative for a step away from it. Because the actor samples its predictions, it has to find out by itself which refinements pay off.

$$ r_t = \log a_{t,y} - \log a_{t-1,y} $$

Discounting trains every depth

Over an episode the rewards telescope, and only the first and the current prediction remain. With a discount $\gamma$, the expected return becomes the log probability of the label at a random depth $N$ drawn from a geometric distribution. Every depth carries weight, so every intermediate prediction is trained to be a good answer. RIC can therefore stop at any step and still return a useful prediction, and the critic of step 4 decides when.

$$ \mathbb{E}\Big[\textstyle\sum_{t\ge1}\gamma^{t-1} r_t\Big] = \mathbb{E}_{N\sim\mathrm{Geom}(1-\gamma)}\big[\log a_{N,y}\big] + \log K $$

Same target, different learning

For labels drawn from the true conditional $q(\cdot \mid x)$, the expected cross-entropy of a stochastic prediction with mean $\mu$ splits into three parts. No model can remove the label ambiguity, while the error of the mean prediction and the cost of random actions can be removed. Maximizing the return drives these two to zero at every depth, so a deterministic RIC policy predicts $q(\cdot \mid x)$, the same target as cross-entropy.

The two objectives differ in how they reach it. Cross-entropy differentiates the loss of each prediction and pushes it toward the label as long as any gap remains, so confidence grows with fitting. RIC learns through the policy gradient, which moves a prediction only while its sampled refinements earn distinguishable returns. This difference in learning is the source of the different dynamics in the results.

$$ \mathbb{E}_{y\sim q,\,a\sim\pi}\big[-\log a_y\big] = \underbrace{H(q)}_{\text{label ambiguity}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}(q\,\Vert\,\mu)}_{\text{mean prediction error}} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[D_F(a_y \,\Vert\, \mu_y)\big]}_{\text{cost of stochastic actions}} $$

The critic decides when to stop

Write $L_t = \mathrm{KL}(q\,\Vert\,a_t)$ for the error of the current prediction. The value of a state compares this error with the expected error after further refinement. The label ambiguity is the same now and later and cancels, so the critic measures only the error that refinement can still remove. Refinement continues while $V(s_t) > 0$ and stops once $V(s_t) \le 0$. An ambiguous input can stop early with an uncertain prediction, and a confident but wrong one keeps refining. The stopping rule comes from the critic RIC already trains, with no ponder penalty or separate halting network.

$$ V^\pi(s_t) = L_t - \mathbb{E}_{N\sim\mathrm{Geom}(1-\gamma),\,\pi}\!\left[L_{t+N} \mid s_t\right] $$

Results

We train RIC with Simple Policy Optimization (RIC-SPO) and compare it with supervised learning (SL) and with recurrent and evidential baselines on ten image classification benchmarks, including noisy human labels and medical images.

  • SL’s validation return declines while RIC’s holds.
  • RIC fits the training set more gradually and generalizes better at the same training accuracy. It keeps improving where supervised training starts to overfit.
  • At every noise level, RIC ends training better calibrated than SL.
  • Cross-entropy ties confidence to fitting, so SL becomes near certain early. RIC builds confidence at its own pace and keeps moderate probabilities on mislabeled examples.
  • SL is overconfident. RIC stays close to calibrated.
  • Across ten datasets, the deterministic readout of RIC favors accuracy and the stochastic readout favors calibration, and together they form the Pareto front.
  • Recurrent baselines trained with cross-entropy are calibrated like SL, so the gain comes from learning from returns.
  • Easy inputs saturate fast. Hard ones stop before reward turns negative.
  • The critic makes the right stop-or-continue call on most states, without any extra halting machinery.

Takeaways

  • RIC offers an alternative way to train classifiers, by reinforcement instead of imitation.
  • Freed from imitation, the model builds confidence at its own pace, and calibration emerges in training.
  • The training dynamics favor models that are more robust to label noise.
  • At deployment, deterministic rollouts favor accuracy and stochastic rollouts favor calibration.
  • Better reinforcement learning algorithms could improve both speed and performance.
